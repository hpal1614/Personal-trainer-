# 04 — Progression: How You Actually Improve

Progressive overload is the law that governs muscle and strength. Without it, you maintain at best. On a cut, progression looks different than in a surplus — your goal shifts from "constantly add weight" to **"hold your numbers and grind out small wins."** Keeping your lifts on a cut *is* a win; it means you're keeping muscle.

---

## The core principle: double progression

For each exercise, you have a **rep range** (e.g., 8–10). Progress like this:

1. Pick a weight you can do for the **bottom** of the range with 1–2 RIR.
2. Each session, try to add **1 rep** to one or more sets.
3. When you hit the **top** of the range on **all** working sets, **add weight** (2.5–5 kg on compounds, smallest jump on isolation) and drop back to the bottom of the range.
4. Repeat.

**Example:** Bench, range 5–8.
- Week 1: 100 kg × 5, 5, 5, 5
- Week 2: 100 kg × 6, 6, 5, 5
- Week 3: 100 kg × 8, 8, 7, 7
- Week 4: 100 kg × 8, 8, 8, 8 → **add weight** → 102.5 kg × 5, 5, 5, 5

Simple, brutal, effective.

---

## Other ways to add overload (when weight stalls)

When the bar stops moving up — which happens in a deficit — you can still progress by:

- **More reps** at the same weight (the primary driver above).
- **More sets** (carefully; only if recovery allows).
- **Better form / fuller ROM** — a cleaner, deeper rep is more stimulus.
- **Slower eccentrics** — a 3-sec negative is harder than a 1-sec one at the same weight.
- **Shorter rest** on isolation work — more density.
- **Better mind-muscle connection** on isolation lifts.

All of these increase the training stimulus without needing a heavier bar.

---

## Autoregulation: train the body you have *today*

You won't have the same energy every session, especially cutting. Use **RIR-based autoregulation**:

- **Feeling strong?** Push the top end, maybe add a rep beyond target.
- **Feeling beat up / underslept / underfed?** Hit your prescribed sets at a slightly higher RIR (stop 2–3 short instead of 1–2). Don't grind to failure when recovery is low — you'll just dig a deeper hole.

The program is a **plan, not a prison.** Auto-regulate around it.

---

## Deloads: planned recovery

Fatigue accumulates. Every **5–6 weeks**, take a **deload week**:

- Keep the same exercises, but use **~50–60% of your normal working weight** OR **cut your sets in half**.
- Keep it crisp and easy — the point is to shed fatigue, not chase a pump.
- You'll come back **stronger**, not weaker. Skipping deloads is how advanced lifters stall and get hurt.

A deload also pairs well with a **diet break** week (see nutrition) — recover the body and the diet at the same time.

---

## Breaking a plateau (decision tree)

A lift hasn't moved in 2–3 weeks. Work through this in order:

1. **Are you sleeping 7–9 hrs?** No → fix that first. It's almost always this.
2. **Are you hitting protein & calories?** No → fix nutrition.
3. **Has it been >5 weeks since a deload?** Yes → deload, then retry.
4. **Is your form breaking down?** Yes → drop weight 10%, rebuild with clean reps.
5. **Still stalled on a cut?** That's often *expected* — maintaining strength in a deficit is success. Don't force PRs when you're under-fueled; save the PR chase for your lean-gain phase.
6. **Genuinely stuck in a surplus?** Swap the exercise variation (e.g., barbell bench → close-grip or incline) for 4–6 weeks. New stimulus breaks the rut.

---

## Track everything

You cannot progressively overload what you don't measure. **Log every working set** — weight, reps, RIR — every session. Use a notebook or an app (Strong, Hevy, etc.). Next session, you open the log and try to beat the last number. That single habit separates people who grow from people who spin their wheels.
